§1 Why this one Carried from Rev A

The competitive read, in short, because it determines what we optimise for. Unchanged since Rev A, and the build has not given us a reason to revisit it.

Seventy-three mini apps have been submitted across both cycles of this competition. I read every one. They include four rotating-savings apps, five bill splitters, six tip jars, seven AI assistants, and a dozen games.

Not one of them touches fiat. Every entry moves value inside the crypto system and stops there. Nobody connects to a bank account, a mobile money account, or cash.

That gap exists because a ramp is not a coding problem. It needs rails, liquidity, and compliance clearance — none of which a team can acquire in a four-week competition. We already have all three, across four corridors, in both directions. That is the entire thesis: our advantage is not the code, and therefore cannot be copied during the event.

Nimiq built OASIS and Fastspot to solve exactly this — a non-custodial bridge between crypto and real money. After five years it reaches one currency, the euro, through one German bank.

We are extending their own unsolved flagship problem into four markets they cannot reach. That framing goes in the submission description and gets said aloud on the community call.

The correction that matters most

Rev C carried a support matrix: a table of which corridors could be cashed into and out of, built by probing the rail once and writing down what it said. It was wrong in kind, not in detail.

The rail serves both directions in every currency it supports. What it had actually told us was which provider was quoting at the moment we asked — a liquidity reading, not a capability. Shipped as a matrix it would have told a Tanzanian user, permanently and in confident type, that their corridor could not be cashed into. That sentence would have become false the next time a provider came online, and nothing in our code would ever have noticed.

So the table is gone. Refusals are classified when they happen: structural (this token is not on this network, this currency is not served), no liquidity (no provider for that amount right now), or unknown, which we show as the rail’s own words rather than paraphrasing a guess. A no-liquidity refusal invites the user to try a different amount. A structural one does not, because it will not change.

The general form of the mistake is worth naming, because it is cheap to make again: we recorded a snapshot of a remote system as though it were a property of that system.

The corridors are not shaped alike

A detail with real UI consequences, found by reading the rail’s institution list per corridor rather than assuming one shape. Nigeria returns 171 banks and no mobile money at all. Uganda returns two mobile-money providers and no banks. Kenya and Tanzania sit between them.

A single “choose your bank” screen serves neither market honestly. The picker takes its labels and its search behaviour from the corridor: searching 171 institutions needs a filter, choosing between two does not, and calling a mobile-money account a bank account is the kind of detail a local user reads as evidence that the product was not built for them.

§3 Architecture Revised in Rev D

A mini app is a web app in a WebView inside Nimiq Pay, talking to injected wallet providers. There is no chain integration work — which is exactly why the chain list is as long as it is.

Cash in NGN / KES / TZS / UGX→ our onramp→ USDT · USDC→ Nimiq Pay wallet
Cash out Nimiq Pay wallet→ USDT · USDC→ our offramp→ NGN / KES / TZS / UGX

Every contract address and decimal in the registry was read from the chain itself rather than from memory or documentation. That is not diligence theatre: USDT and USDC on BNB Smart Chain carry eighteen decimals where every other chain uses six. Treating that pair as six-decimal tokens sends a trillion times the intended amount, and it is the single most expensive assumption in the codebase. It is also exactly the kind of fact a language model recalls confidently and wrongly, which is why the rule is to read it.

The rail

Both directions are one endpoint. POST /v2/sender/orders takes polymorphic source and destination objects — {type: "crypto"} into {type: "fiat"} is a cash-out, the reverse is a cash-in — and returns a providerAccount holding either the address to send stablecoin to or the bank details to pay into, with a validUntil. Rates come from the public GET /v2/rates/{network}/{from}/{amount}/{to}, which needs no key. Progress arrives as payment_order.* webhooks signed with X-Paycrest-Signature, an HMAC-SHA256 of the raw body — verified against the exact bytes received, because verifying against a re-serialised object fails on key order and the tempting fix is to loosen the check until it passes, at which point the signature is decoration.

Our 0.5% rides on the rail’s own senderFeePercent rather than being arithmetic we do. It is charged where the rail charges it, appears on the rail’s record of the order, and shows on the receipt as the rail reported it — so our receipt cannot disagree with the ledger it describes.

Cash-out needs gas, and the wallet does not have any

The problem no revision saw coming, found by trying to sell after buying. A cash-in lands stablecoin in a wallet holding no native token at all. The very next thing anyone does is try to send it back out, and they cannot pay the network fee. The wallet says insufficient gas, which is a dead end a user cannot act on and which sounds like our bug.

The server now covers it: it reads the wallet’s balance over its own RPC, sends the shortfall, and waits for it to land before the app asks for a signature. On Polygon that is 0.05 POL, about two cents, once per order, and only ever to the address that owns that order. It is a faucet, so it is almost entirely refusal — one payout per order, only the shortfall, never above a per-chain ceiling, never past a daily budget, and priced per chain because half a POL is twenty cents and half an ETH is a car.

Three more attractive options were rejected, and the reasoning is recorded because each one looks like the obvious answer:

ApproachWhy it looked rightWhy not
Pimlico / ERC-4337A paymaster is purpose-built for exactly thisSponsors UserOperations from smart accounts. Nimiq Pay’s wallet is an EOA, and a 4337 paymaster cannot pay for an EOA’s transaction. EIP-7702 changes this in principle, but needs a signing method injected providers are even less likely to expose.
OpenGSNIt is what Nimiq’s own wallet uses for gasless USDCRelays call the target through a Forwarder, so the target must be ERC-2771 aware. USDT and USDC are not — a relayed transfer would move the Forwarder’s tokens. Nimiq solved this by deploying their own recipient contract; we would need one per chain.
EIP-2612 permitBoth tokens answer PERMIT_TYPEHASH; no contract to deployThe better product, and still the right next build. It needs an EIP-712 signature from the injected provider that nothing documents, and two to three days. Neither was available on the last morning.

All three share one gate: the wallet must sign typed data. The seeded fee does not care, works on all five chains and both tokens today, and stays useful as the fallback if the signature route later works. EIP-3009 transferWithAuthorization is the version to build next — one call instead of two, and the authorisation names the recipient, so the signature cannot be used to move funds anywhere else.

The fee bug underneath the gas bug

Worth recording because the diagnosis was wrong twice. After seeding the fee, cash-out still failed — and the wallet held 0.0506 POL against 0.0192 needed, a 2.6× surplus, with a transaction count of zero. It had never broadcast anything.

We were sending no fee fields at all and leaving the wallet to choose. Polygon’s validators enforce a 25 gwei minimum priority fee; a wallet carrying Ethereum-shaped defaults picks one or two, and the network refuses the transaction as underpriced. The refusal mentions the fee, so it reads as a shortfall on a wallet that has plenty. The app now prices its own transactions: the node’s suggested priority fee raised to the chain’s floor, inside a cap of twice the base fee.

What the platform gives us free

Stablecoins are exposed through the standard injected window.ethereum provider with no additional setup, which is the whole reason five chains cost about as much as one. Nimiq-native calls come through @nimiq/mini-app-sdk. Keys never leave the wallet, and every sensitive action raises a native confirmation dialog we cannot bypass or restyle — good for us, because it means we are never in the custody path.

Deeplinks open the app directly inside Nimiq Pay from any domain — nimiqpay://miniapp?url= or https://nimpay.app/miniapps/open/. That is how a user hands the app to another user, and it costs us nothing to support.

Why NIM is not in the design

Nimiq’s L1 is not EVM-compatible, so there is no AMM and no DEX. The only non-custodial path to NIM is Fastspot, which quotes against euro through a single regulated German bank, with API access behind a waitlist. There is no programmatic route from our corridors to NIM. We settle in stablecoin and forgo the NIM bonus points deliberately — the rules permit “USDT, NIM, or both.”

§4 The state machine is the product Revised in Rev D

The single most important engineering decision in this brief, and the one that has survived every revision untouched. Rev D adds the only thing it was missing: evidence that it runs.

Build the transaction state machine, the status view, and the receipt first — before the happy path, before any styling. The scoring makes the reason explicit: “Error handling — failures are caught and explained clearly instead of crashing or freezing.” A game with a rough edge is forgivable. A payments app where someone’s money disappears into a spinner is the worst possible thing to put in front of a judge, and the worst possible thing to do to a real user.

quoted→ confirmed→ submitted→ settling→ completed
quote_expired rejected refunding failed_refunded failed_manual stalled

refunding is not in Rev A’s list because Rev A was written before we knew the rail. It sits between a failure and the money landing back, and it is not the same thing as settling — one means your transaction is working, the other means it failed and you are being repaid. Conflating them is precisely the “caught and explained clearly” failure the rubric names. failed_manual surfaces a reference the user can quote and a way to reach a human. stalled appears automatically after a settlement window rather than leaving anyone staring at settling indefinitely.

One state machine serves both legs, and that is what made full scope arithmetic rather than optimism. The states are direction-agnostic — settling is settling whether we are waiting on a bank payout or an inbound deposit — so the second direction was a set of adapters instead of a second product.

§5 Engineering priorities, and where they come from Carried from Rev A

The scoring rubric is a hundred points across five categories. It is unusually explicit, so we aimed at it directly.

Functionality & usefulness45
Nimiq Pay integration25
Real usage15
Design & UX10
Builder promotion5

Reliability outranks features. Forty-five points sit on a category whose sub-criteria are core feature, error handling, speed, stability and completeness. Completeness is one of the five and it is the case for shipping both legs. The other four are the case for §4 going first.

Integration depth is worth more than polish. Twenty-five points reward using the platform properly — native dialogs, real localisation, the device identifier, deeplinks — against ten for visual design. Most entrants will call listAccounts() and stop. Five chains and two tokens is the same argument: it is depth in the platform, and it cost a registry rather than a rewrite.

Real usage is measured, not claimed. Fifteen points require people actually transacting before the deadline, which is the one category a late ship genuinely costs us.

What the compression actually cost

Rev A budgeted eight build days and shipped on the 13th. We had five and shipped on the 14th at wider scope than Rev A described. The arithmetic worked because Rev A was written single-threaded, and because one state machine served both legs.

The bill came due elsewhere. The on-chain NIM receipt is the one scope item that did not land, and the gas problem in §3 surfaced on the last morning rather than the first — found by using the product in the order a real user does, which is the test no amount of unit coverage substitutes for. Neither was a planning failure exactly, but both are the shape of thing a week of float would have absorbed.

§7 Risks Revised in Rev D

Five of Rev C’s risks have now either fired or closed. They are kept rather than deleted, with what actually happened, because a risk register that only lists the ones still open is a register nobody learns from.

Fired

The ETHOnline collision

Rated Medium in Rev A and scoped to a single date. It took days one through four instead — the foundation days.

OutcomeAbsorbed by parallelising and by one state machine serving both legs. The four days did not come back; the NIM receipt is what they cost.

Fired

There is no sandbox — every test is real money

New in Rev C, and correct. Paycrest is mainnet-only with a $0.50 floor. Every branch of §4 we wanted to prove cost real currency across a real rail.

OutcomeThe cap and kill switch landed before the first spike call and both fail closed — an unset cap means $50, not unlimited; an unparseable kill switch means on. Total spend proving both legs was under $5. Two settled orders in §4 are what that bought.

Fired

Cash-out is unaffordable in a freshly funded wallet

New in Rev D, and in no earlier revision because no earlier revision had used the product in the order a user does. A cash-in leaves stablecoin and no native token; the next action costs gas the wallet cannot pay.

OutcomeServer-side fee seeding, shipped this morning — §3. Compounded by a second, unrelated bug underneath it: we sent no fee fields and let the wallet pick, below Polygon’s enforced 25 gwei floor. The first diagnosis was wrong because the symptom named the wrong cause.

Live

Two corridors have a single provider between them

The live rates endpoint returns two providerIds for NGN and exactly one for KES and TZS — the same one. If that provider goes quiet, two of our four corridors go with it, and no retry on our side helps.

MitigationNGN launched first on provider redundancy rather than rate. A quiet provider now surfaces as a no-liquidity refusal in the user’s own words (§2) rather than as a generic failure, and the stall window is tuned so it does not read as slow settlement.

Live

The rail’s account verifier is unreliable

New in Rev D. /v2/verify-account — which checks a bank account resolves to a real name before anyone commits — returns a Cloudflare 504 consistently. It is theirs, not ours, and it is the one dependency we cannot route around.

MitigationDegraded rather than blocking. “We cannot check right now” is treated as distinct from “that account is wrong”: an outage offers use it anyway, a real rejection blocks. Conflating them either waves a mistyped account number through or halts every transfer until the verifier returns. The rail re-checks at order time regardless.

Live

The fee address is configured outside our code

New in Rev D, and the only open item touching money. Our 0.5% is paid to a senderFeeAddress set in the rail’s dashboard. It is not in the repository, not in .env, and not under review — so nothing in our process would catch it being wrong.

MitigationConfirm the address is one the team lead controls before further volume. Both settled orders in §4 already paid into it.

Live

The gas faucet is a faucet

New in Rev D. A hot key on the API server that sends money to addresses on request is, stated plainly, an attack surface we did not have last week.

MitigationBounded on every axis: one payout per order, only to the address that owns that order, only the shortfall, never above a per-chain ceiling, never past a daily budget, and refusing rather than underfunding when gas spikes. The wallet holds about $5. Worst case is the loss of $5 and a corridor that asks users to hold gas again.

Closed

Shared rails code with the ETHOnline project

Elevated in Rev B on the reasoning that lifting a working integration would look extremely reasonable at 2am on the 13th.

OutcomeHeld. Its own repository, its own rails client, written fresh, with 197 tests of its own.

§8 Definition of done Revised in Rev D
